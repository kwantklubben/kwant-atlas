### 1. Intuition & Practical Objective

Page 01 established the identity $\text{NI} = \text{CFO} + \text{Accruals}$ and argued the accrual term is the fragile part. This page is the *empirical* case: **Sloan (1996) proved that the fragility is both real and mispriced.** It is the founding paper of the entire earnings-quality literature and the reason "accruals" is a factor in every serious quantamental screen.

The argument has two independent legs, and you should keep them separate because they fall separately:

1. **A statistical claim (not about markets at all):** the earnings generated by *accruals* are **less persistent** than the earnings generated by *cash flows*. If this year's profit is $1 and it arrived as cash, about 85 cents of it recurs next year; if it arrived as an accrual, only about 76 cents recurs. Accruals are, on average, lower-quality earnings - that is a property of accounting, not of investors.
2. **A market claim (about mispricing):** investors do **not** fully discount for that difference. They behave as if a dollar of accrual earnings equals a dollar of cash earnings - Sloan calls this **"earnings fixation."** The consequence is a trading strategy: buy the firms whose earnings are *all cash*, short the firms whose earnings are *all accruals*, and you earn a large abnormal return while the mistake unwinds.

The practical objective: understand that **accruals are not merely a forensic tool, they are a return predictor** - and understand the three competing explanations for *why*, because they imply different things about whether the effect should still be there.

Three candidate explanations (Chan, Jegadeesh & Lakonishok 2006 sharpen exactly this):

- **Manipulation hypothesis** - managers deliberately inflate accruals; the market is fooled. If true, only the *discretionary* part of accruals should predict returns.
- **Extrapolative-bias hypothesis** - investors over-extrapolate the sales/earnings growth that mechanically drives working-capital accruals. If true, the *nondiscretionary* part predicts returns too.
- **Delayed-reaction hypothesis** - the information in accruals is public but absorbed slowly, exactly like post-earnings-announcement drift. If true, the anomaly is a drift phenomenon and should decay as arbitrage capital and data availability improve.

### 2. Mathematical Ground Truth & Derivations

**Setup (Sloan's variables, all scaled by average total assets).** Let $E_t$ be earnings performance (income from continuing operations / avg TA), $ACC_t$ the accrual component, $CF_t$ the cash-flow component, with $E_t = ACC_t + CF_t$.

**Hypothesis 1 - differential persistence.** The naive model constrains both components to revert at the same rate:

$$
E_{t+1} = c_0 + \alpha_1 E_t + v_{t+1}. \tag{4}
$$

The correctly specified model frees them:

$$
E_{t+1} = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1\,ACC_t + \gamma_2\,CF_t + v_{t+1}, \qquad \boxed{\gamma_1 < \gamma_2}. \tag{5}
$$

Sloan's pooled estimates: $\alpha_1 = 0.841$ (earnings are mean-reverting but slow, far from a random walk), and

$$
\gamma_1 = 0.765 \quad (\text{accruals}), \qquad \gamma_2 = 0.855 \quad (\text{cash flow}).
$$

An $F$-test rejects $\gamma_1=\gamma_2$ at $F=614$. Industry-by-industry, $\gamma_1<\gamma_2$ in 86% of industries. Using decile **ranks** instead of raw values (to kill outlier influence) the gap widens: $0.565$ vs $0.838$.

**Hypothesis 2 - market inefficiency.** The returns test writes abnormal returns as the market's *mis-reaction* to the components. Under the rational-expectations benchmark abnormal returns are zero in expectation; the misspecification is that the market uses a single combined coefficient instead of $\gamma_1, \gamma_2$. The prediction is directional and clean:

> **Firms with relatively high (low) accruals experience negative (positive) future abnormal returns.**

**The trading strategy.** Rank all firms annually on the magnitude of the accrual component, form deciles, hold one year (return cumulation starts four months after fiscal year-end, by which time almost all statements are public). Size-adjusted abnormal returns (Sloan Table 6):

| Portfolio | Year +1 | Year +2 | Year +3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lowest-accrual decile (long) | **+4.9%** ($t=2.65$) | +1.6% ($t=1.17$) | +0.7% ($t=0.55$) |
| Highest-accrual decile (short) | **−5.5%** ($t=-3.98$) | −3.2% ($t=-2.25$) | −2.2% ($t=-1.61$) |
| **Hedge (long low, short high)** | **+10.4%** ($t=4.71$) | **+4.8%** ($t=3.15$) | +2.9% ($t=1.64$, n.s.) |

Jensen-alpha returns (Ibbotson technique) are "generally consistent." The effect **concentrates in the first year and decays** - and Sloan finds roughly a quarter of the abnormal return is clustered around subsequent earnings announcements, i.e. it is a drift, not a one-time repricing.

**Sample scale.** 40,679 firm-years, 1962–1991, NYSE/AMEX - a 30-year panel, which is why the result is treated as sturdy rather than a single-decade artifact.

> **Sign convention warning (the most common error on this page).** Sloan sorts on the **magnitude of the accrual *component***, and the accrual component is *positively* related to earnings but *negatively* related to cash flow (rank correlation of accruals with cash flows is **−0.53**). So "high accrual" means "high reported earnings, low cash behind them." Do not confuse it with "high accrual *ratio*" sorting - Sloan notes the rank correlation between the accrual component and the accruals/earnings ratio is 0.94, so the variants are near-identical, which is why several implementations are all defensible.

### 3. Computational Implementation - reconstructing the anomaly

Stdlib only. The script builds a panel from Sloan's own data-generating process, runs both regressions via a hand-rolled OLS (normal equations + Gaussian elimination - no numpy), sorts into accrual deciles, and reports the hedge returns. It should recover $\gamma_1\approx0.765$, $\gamma_2\approx0.855$.



The recovered coefficients ($0.765$, $0.857$) match Sloan's pooled estimates (0.765, 0.855) to the second decimal (0.857 vs 0.855, within sampling noise) - the estimator and the DGP agree, which is the point of the exercise.

### 4. Failure Modes & First-Principles Breakdowns

1. **The anomaly decays, and may not pay after costs.** The hedge return falls from 10.4% (year 1) to 4.8% (year 2) to 2.9% (year 3, insignificant). Accrual-based strategies are high-turnover and load on small, illiquid, high-idiosyncratic-risk stocks - Sloan himself checks portfolio betas and finds the high-accrual decile is *riskier*, so part of the raw spread may be compensation, not free money. A paper's 10.4% is not a strategy's 10.4%.
2. **Correlated measurement error and look-ahead.** Accruals computed from *restated* Compustat data leak the future; the paper's own point-in-time discipline (4-month lag, as-reported data) is what makes the 10.4% a *tradable* number. Any backtest that ignores this will overstate it. See [[fundamentals-accounting/data-sources-and-corporate-data/index|Data Sources & Corporate Data]].
3. **The components are not independent.** Accruals and cash flows are strongly negatively correlated (rank corr $−0.53$), so "long low-accrual, short high-accrual" is close to "long high-cash-flow, short low-cash-flow." Before claiming an *accrual* effect, residualise one component on the other - otherwise you may be measuring a cash-flow anomaly wearing an accrual costume.
4. **Trend, not level.** Accruals revert, so the strongest signal is a *rise* in accruals (a deterioration), not a *level*. A firm with structurally high accruals (a fast-growing lender-like business model) is not a manipulation candidate - Chan et al. show the nondiscretionary component predicts returns too, which is inconsistent with the pure-manipulation reading.
