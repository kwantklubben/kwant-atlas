**Basic Prerequisites:** none beyond high-school probability. A willingness to distrust a good-looking equity curve is required.

### 1. Intuition & Practical Objective

This page builds the *why* of backtesting hygiene with **no statistics background needed**. The objective is one idea: **a backtest reports the best result of a search, and the best of many random results always looks good - so a backtest is evidence of nothing until you say how hard you searched.**

Start with the dumbest question: *what is a backtest?* Naively, "a historical simulation of how a strategy would have performed." But nobody runs exactly one backtest. You tried a 20-day moving average, then 50, then 200; you added a stop loss, removed it; you restricted the universe; you flipped the sign. Your final report contains the survivor of that whole process. The number you printed is not the strategy's return - it is the *maximum* of a set of returns you generated by fishing.

Three steps:

1. **Random data always contains patterns.** A fair coin flipped 1,000 times contains runs, streaks, and "seasonality" - *somewhere*. A researcher who scans enough of these patterns finds a rule that "predicts" them. The rule has zero power on the next 1,000 flips, because there was never a signal to find.

2. **The winner is selected *for* its luck.** Suppose 1,000 strategies have zero true skill. Their estimated Sharpe ratios are noisy draws around zero. The *best* of 1,000 noisy draws is not near zero - it is a large positive number, purely by the arithmetic of taking a maximum. That is the **winner's curse**: the strategies you select are disproportionately the ones whose noise happened to be favorable.

3. **You only see the survivors.** Researchers, journals, and fund databases publish winners. The 999 losers are invisible ("file drawer"). So the observed distribution of published backtests is *itself* a selection - biased exactly toward overstatement.

> **The one-sentence essence.** "A backtest is the maximum of $N$ trials; unless you account for $N$ and the chance-driven height of that maximum, a beautiful equity curve is indistinguishable from 1,000 coin flips."

### 2. Mathematical Ground Truth & Derivations

**The coin-flip demonstration (Bailey & López de Prado 2014).** Toss a fair coin ten times. A sequence like $+,+,+,+,+,-,-,-,-,-$ appears roughly once every $2^{10}=1024$ attempts. A researcher declares the optimal betting rule: "bet heads for the first five tosses, tails for the last five." That is a *seasonal strategy*. On the next ten tosses ($-,-,+,-,+,+,-,-,+,-$) it wins 5 and loses 5 - the rule was fit to a random pattern that did not recur. **Nothing broke; there was never a signal.** This is backtest overfitting in its purest form.

**Where the exaggeration comes from - arithmetic, not psychology.** Let the $N$ trials' estimated Sharpe ratios be $\{\widehat{SR}_n\}$, drawn around a true mean of zero (no skill) with cross-trial variance $V[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]$. The reported statistic is $M=\max_n\widehat{SR}_n$. Order statistics tell us its *expected* value is **positive and increasing in $N$**, even though the truth is zero:

$$
\mathbb{E}[M]>0\quad\text{strictly, and}\quad \mathbb{E}[M]\uparrow\infty \text{ as } N\to\infty.
$$

The precise size of that drift is the subject of [[pillars/01-quantitative-research/backtesting-hygiene/03-the-multiple-testing-problem|03 · The Multiple-Testing Problem]], and the deflation it demands is [[pillars/01-quantitative-research/backtesting-hygiene/04-deflated-sharpe-ratio|04 · The Deflated Sharpe Ratio]].

**In-sample vs out-of-sample - the two words that carry all the weight.** Split history into an *in-sample* (IS) part you learn on and an *out-of-sample* (OOS) part you validate on. The gap between IS and OOS performance is the **performance degradation**, and in a real series with memory it is typically *negative*, not merely zero (a fitted pattern gets actively "undone").

**Why "I only backtested once" is almost never true.** Every choice - universe, dates, cost model, rebalance frequency, data vendor - was tried in some form. The honest question is never "did I overfit?" (yes, somewhat) but "how many *effective* independent trials did this result survive?"

### 3. Computational Implementation - watch a coin become a "strategy"

Two stdlib experiments. **Experiment A** shows a pure-noise rule scoring a perfect-looking win-rate; **Experiment B** shows the best of $N$ zero-skill strategies *growing* with $N$. Both use only `random`, so the mechanism is fully transparent.



Two messages. **A:** with 1,000 searches on short samples, the best of pure coin flips "wins" 85% of the time - the "strategy" is a coin (with 1,000 flips each, the law of large numbers dilutes the fluke back toward 0.5, but real backtests are *short* relative to their search space, so the short-sample regime is the realistic one). **B:** with **zero skill** and only 3 years of daily data, the best of 1,000 strategies still shows an **annualized Sharpe $\approx1.9$** - and the exact order-statistic formula predicts it (1.879 vs 1.882). There is no signal; the Sharpe is the *height of a maximum*.

### 4. Failure Modes & First-Principles Breakdowns

1. **"But my strategy makes economic sense."** A *story* attached to a pattern does not turn the pattern into a signal. Narrative and multiple-testing are orthogonal: a genuinely motivated hypothesis still has to clear a threshold that accounts for how the data were searched. (Motivation reduces the *effective* $N$; it does not set it to one.)
2. **Confusing IS with OOS.** If you tune any parameter on the OOS window - even "just the stop loss" - it is no longer out of sample. The single most common self-deception.
3. **Survivorship in the sample itself.** Backtesting on today's index constituents tells you how today's winners behaved *after being selected as winners*. This is a silent upward bias that has nothing to do with multiple testing.
4. **The "one trial" fallacy.** Believing a single holdout validates a strategy ignores that the holdout was itself reached after many discarded attempts - see [[pillars/01-quantitative-research/backtesting-hygiene/02-why-backtests-lie|02 · Why Backtests Lie]] and the holdout critique on page 03.
